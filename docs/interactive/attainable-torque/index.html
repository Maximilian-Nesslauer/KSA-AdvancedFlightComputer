Attainable torque sets

How an RCS or aero controller can tell touchdown guidance what torque it can deliver, without guidance knowing whether the craft has thrusters, grid fins or flaps.

This is a design sketch. AFC's allocators on main work differently.

  • Fins: AeroTorqueAllocator solves a linear programme over each surface's torque curve, swept at rest and every 15° to its limits and split into straight segments. It starts from where each surface is now and goes only as far as its rate reaches by the next step. A small price per degree away from rest and per degree moved keeps idle surfaces home and stops them swapping work. Refinement passes then correct the answer where a curve bends.
  • RCS: the thrusters are backup columns in that same programme, priced by their propellant, so the fins take every axis they can and the RCS gives the rest.
  • Gimbals: KsaTvcAllocator.Solve in TvcAllocator.cs, its own solve. It is a regularised minimum-norm inverse, u = Bᵀ(BBᵀ + εI)⁻¹τ, on the 3 × 2N gimbal torque Jacobian. Roll, the weak axis, is allocated last in the travel the others leave, and gimbals that reach a stop are held there while the free ones carry the rest. It delivers the 6-DOF plan's gimbal torque.
  • AttitudeController spends its torque on the fins and the RCS only. It does not use the engine.
  • The 6-DOF plan carries one deflection per fin, in degrees, bounded by the fin's range and rate. It has no λ.

The attainable set drawn below is still the right picture of what those allocators can reach together. The Allocate button runs this page's own small least-squares solve, not AFC's.

The equation

τ = c + G λ−1 ≤ λi ≤ 1
τ
A torque the controller can deliver, in body axes (roll, pitch, yaw). On this page it is divided by the craft's inertia, so it reads as angular acceleration in rad/s², and the air, the gimbals and the RCS compare directly.
λi
Where actuator i sits in its travel: −1 at one stop, +1 at the other, 0 halfway. For an RCS axis, −1 is full torque one way and +1 full torque the other. Guidance plans with λ and never sees degrees or part names.
Gi
Column i of G: the torque actuator i adds as it moves from halfway to its +1 stop. Its direction says which axes it turns and its length says how hard. A grid fin's column points partly in pitch or yaw and partly in roll.
c
The torque with every actuator at halfway. It is zero for anything that moves equally both ways (RCS, grid fins at ±20°). It is not zero for a one-sided surface: a flap travels from folded to open, so its halfway point is half open and already pushes.
Where it comes from. Surface i moves its deflection δi between two stops, and the torque it adds is about bi δi, where bi = ∂τ/∂δi is measured by the aero sweep. Write each deflection as its midpoint plus a fraction of its half-range: δᵢ = mᵢ + hᵢ·λᵢ mᵢ = (δmax + δmin)/2, hᵢ = (δmax − δmin)/2 c = Σ bᵢ·mᵢ Gᵢ = bᵢ·hᵢ Each actuator on its own reaches a line segment of torques. All of them together reach every sum of one point from each segment, which is a solid with flat faces (a zonotope). Three RCS axes give a box. Four grid fins give a slanted shape. The planner adds λ as a few bounded variables per node, so each subproblem stays a cone program.

Mach, angle of attack and orientation

It looks hard, but the code already does it for the air's own moment. BodyAeroTable stores the body-axis moment coefficients Cl, Cm, Cn over a grid of Mach, α and β, fitted with one cubic B-spline that gives the value and its gradient together. The surfaces would ride on the same grid.

  • Orientation is α and β. What matters to the air is where the flow comes from in body axes, and the table already defines that as two tilts from engine-first (missile-style, no singularity at zero). The craft is held at a fixed roll, so the body axes are known for any pointing.
  • Dynamic pressure comes out front. The table stores coefficients (torque over q S L), so the torque is q times a table read. That is why the shapes here grow with the dynamic-pressure slider.
  • Mach and α change the shape. Each grid node stores c (3 numbers) and G (3 × k numbers for k surfaces), next to the 3 it stores now. Grid fins lose effect around Mach 1 and at high α; flaps barely work engine-first and work best broadside. Try both on the sliders.

Sweep

At each (Mach, α, β) node, sample the craft as now, then with each surface at each stop: two more samples per surface per node. That gives bi as a full-travel slope, so the stops are exact even if a fin is not linear in between. The sweep has to be off the main thread first (#223).

Plan

Each node reads c and G at the reference trajectory's Mach, α and β, times its q. G(x)λ is a product of the state and λ, so SCvx linearises it about the last iterate, as it does the air's moment now. The trust region keeps that honest.

Fly

The controller reads the same table at the current state, solves for the deflections, and reports the torque it actually got back to guidance. On main that solve is AeroTorqueAllocator's linear programme (see the note at the top).

Where it gets genuinely hard

ProblemWhyWhat to do
Belly-flop attitudesThe body tables cover small tilts from engine-first, and touchdown fades body aero out beyond 20–28°. A belly-flopping ship hands over near 90° and flips in the burn.Whole-sphere surface tables, plus a flip phase or a touchdown problem that allows large attitudes. Separate work.
Surfaces shadowing each otherMoving one fin at a time measures each alone. At high α a forward surface can blank one behind it, so the effects don't add.Check it by sweeping pairs on a test craft; add pair terms only if it matters.
Surfaces push tooFins add drag and side force. Flaps are most of a belly-flopper's drag.Store a force column beside each torque column, so force is also linear in λ. Leave it out at first for grid fins.
Slew ratesFlaps move slowly. A plan that slams λ end to end can't be flown.Bound how fast λ changes per node, as the 6-DOF plan now does for each fin's deflection and for the gimbals' slew.

Where the method comes from

The shape on this page is the attainable moment set from aircraft control allocation. Aircraft with many control surfaces have used it since the early 1990s: the flight control law asks for a moment, and a separate allocator works out the deflections.

  • W. C. Durham, Constrained Control Allocation, J. Guidance, Control, and Dynamics 16(4), 1993. Defines the set of moments reachable within deflection limits as the image of the deflection box. NASA NTRS record
  • T. A. Johansen and T. I. Fossen, Control allocation — A survey, Automatica 49(5), 2013. Covers aircraft, ships and vehicles, with saturation, rate limits and secondary costs. PDF on CORE
  • Tabulating control derivatives over Mach, α and β, scaled by dynamic pressure, is how aircraft aero databases are normally built. Here it reuses the layout of AFC's own BodyAeroTable.

Two parts are my own proposal for this problem, not taken from a paper: putting λ into the SCvx plan as bounded variables per node, and the centre-plus-columns report as the interface between guidance and the controllers. Allocation papers usually use the set inside one control step, not as something a trajectory optimiser plans against.
